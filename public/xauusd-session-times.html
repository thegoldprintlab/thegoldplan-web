XAUUSD session times: when gold actually moves

Gold is open for roughly 23 hours a day, five days a week. The other hours are the part most traders never plan for. This page lists the sessions in UTC, so the numbers mean the same thing in Kuala Lumpur, London and New York.

Every time below is UTC. When London and New York shift to daylight saving, their windows move one hour earlier in UTC and everything else on this page stays put. That single detail causes more confusion than the session hours themselves.

The gold trading day in one line

XAUUSD opens Sunday around 22:00 to 23:00 UTC and closes Friday around 21:00 to 22:00 UTC. In between, trading pauses once a day for roughly an hour, usually somewhere around 21:00 to 22:00 UTC. That pause is the daily break, and on some brokers you will see it as 23:00 to 00:00 or 00:00 to 01:00 because they display their own server time.

So the tradeable week is about 23 hours per day multiplied by five, minus the weekend gap. Gold does not have a session where the price is frozen and orders are ignored, but it does have an hour where liquidity thins out badly, and that hour matters if you hold a position through it.

Checking

Short version: the highest-liquidity window for gold is the London and New York overlap, about 13:00 to 17:00 UTC in winter and 12:00 to 16:00 UTC in summer. Spreads are tightest there, and the moves are the cleanest of the day.

Why the London and New York overlap is the window that counts

For about four hours a day, both the London and New York desks are active at the same time. On gold that shows up as narrower spreads, more size available at each price level, and moves that follow through instead of stabbing both ways.

The practical difference is not that gold only moves during the overlap. It is that a stop placed in the overlap is less likely to be taken out by a spread widening, and a breakout there is less likely to be a fake. Outside it, especially in the hours before the London open, a $3 move can look like a setup and turn out to be one order sweeping an empty book.

If you only have one block of screen time per day and gold is the instrument, the overlap is the block to take. If your schedule forces you into the Asian session, lower your expectations for range and size down accordingly. A 15 pip stop that works at 13:00 UTC is a coin flip at 02:00 UTC.

The daily break and rollover: why gold holders feel it

Once a day, around 21:00 to 22:00 UTC, the market pauses while the trading day rolls over. Two things happen to gold positions in that hour, and both cost money if you are holding.

Spread widening

Liquidity providers step away during rollover. The spread on XAUUSD, already the widest of the major instruments, can widen to several times its normal size for a few minutes. A position that is comfortably in profit at 20:55 UTC can show a paper loss at 21:15 UTC without the price having moved at all. Stops sitting close to price are at real risk of being hit by the spread rather than the market.

Swap fees

Positions held through the rollover get charged or credited swap. Gold swap can be substantial relative to the size of the position, particularly on long holdings, and it is charged per night whether or not the trade is working. A three day hold can quietly give back more than the trade made if the swap is against you. Wednesday rollover is often triple swap, because it settles the weekend.

The consequence is simple: if your plan is intraday, close before the break. If your plan is to hold, then the swap and the widened spread are part of the cost of the trade and belong in your numbers before you enter, not after.

Server time is not UTC: the conversion that wrecks journals

Most MT4 and MT5 brokers run their platform clock on EET or EEST, which is UTC+2 in winter and UTC+3 in summer. That means the daily candle on your chart closes at 00:00 server time, not 00:00 UTC. When you export a statement and group trades by day, you are grouping by the broker's day, which starts two or three hours earlier than the UTC day.

Two traders with the same trades can therefore show different daily results, different win rates per session, and different "best hour" statistics, all because one of them never converted. If you compare your journal to someone else's, or to a strategy write-up that uses UTC, convert first.

The conversion itself is dull: subtract 2 hours in winter, 3 hours in summer, from server time to get UTC. The trap is that the offset changes twice a year, on dates that depend on the broker, and charts produced either side of that change will be off by an hour in the analytics. Note the offset on the trade, or keep the journal in UTC, and the problem disappears.

Picking a session and writing it into the plan

Session choice is a planning decision, not a market prediction. Pick the block you can actually watch, then let everything else in the plan follow from it.

1. Choose one primary block and name it in UTC

Write it down as a UTC window next to your local time, for example: primary window 13:00 to 16:00 UTC, which is 21:00 to 00:00 in Kuala Lumpur. Put both in the plan so a change in daylight saving does not silently move your trading hours.

2. Cap the session, not the trade count

A session limit is easier to keep than a trade limit because it is a clock rather than a decision. Two hours of screen time with no more than three setups is a rule you can hold on a bad day. "Trade less" is not.

3. Tag every trade with the session it was taken in

This is the only way to learn whether your edge is real or a schedule accident. Most traders who tag by session discover their losses cluster in the hours they were tired, not the hours the market was difficult. Two months of tags answers a question no amount of chart study will.

4. Set the daily loss limit around the session, not the calendar day

If you trade one session, a single loss limit for that session is enough. If you trade London and then New York, decide in advance whether the London loss carries into New York. Most blown accounts are the second session trying to repair the first.
