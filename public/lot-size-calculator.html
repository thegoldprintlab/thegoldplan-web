How the lot size is calculated

On XAUUSD one standard lot is 100 troy ounces, so a $1.00 move in the gold price is worth $100 per lot. That single fact drives the whole calculation:

lots = risk in dollars ÷ (stop distance in price × 100)

A $1,000 account risking 1% has a $10 budget. With a 30-pip stop (a $3.00 price distance), the calculation is 10 ÷ (3 × 100) = 0.033 lots. Rounded down to the broker step, that is 0.03 lots — and the real risk is $9, not $10.

Why the lot is rounded down, never up

Rounding 0.033 up to 0.04 quietly turns a 1% risk into a 1.2% risk. Do that on every trade and the account bleeds a fifth more than the plan says. Rounding down costs a few cents of potential profit and keeps the plan honest.

Why risk first, lot second

Most traders pick a lot size out of habit — 0.10, or whatever they used last time — then place the stop wherever the chart allows. That reverses the only part of the trade they actually control. On a $1,000 account, a 0.10 lot with a 30-pip stop risks $30: three times the intended budget, and three full losses in a row is 9% of the account gone.

Sizing from risk means the stop is decided by the chart and the lot is decided by arithmetic. The chart stays honest and the account stays predictable. Once the trade is placed, the next step is to log it — the lot size is one decision, and the pattern across a hundred trades is what actually costs or pays.

What this calculator deliberately leaves out

  • Spread and commission. They widen the effective stop. On a 2-pip spread your real risk is slightly higher than shown.
  • Slippage. Gold gaps through stops during news. Assume the fill is worse than the plan.
  • Correlated positions. Two gold trades in the same direction are one risk, not two.

Common questions

Is a pip on gold 0.01 or 0.10?

Brokers disagree, and both labels are in use. This calculator treats 1 pip as a $0.10 price move, which is the common convention. If your broker calls a $0.01 move a pip, switch the unit to Price ($) and enter the distance directly — the result is identical because sizing only ever uses the price distance.

What lot size should I use on a $500 account?

At 1% risk that is a $5 budget. With a 30-pip stop the answer is 0.01 lots — the minimum. On a $500 account, any stop wider than about 50 pips cannot be traded at 1% risk with a standard account. That is a signal to widen the account or tighten the setup, not to raise the risk.

Does leverage change the lot size?

No. Leverage changes the margin your broker locks, not the money you lose when the stop is hit. It only matters for whether the position fits in free margin — which is why it sits under Advanced.

Why is the lot size rounded down?

Rounding up quietly turns a 1% risk into a larger one. Rounding down to the broker step keeps the position inside the risk budget at the cost of a few cents of potential profit.
