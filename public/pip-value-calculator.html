How the pip value is calculated

One standard lot of XAUUSD is 100 troy ounces, so the value of a price move is the move times 100 times your lot size:

value = price move × 100 × lots

A $0.10 move on 1.0 lot is 0.10 × 100 × 1 = $10. The same move on 0.05 lots is 0.10 × 100 × 0.05 = $0.50. A $0.01 move is one tenth of that, so $1 per lot. That is the entire table, and no pip label can change it.

Why pip value matters more on gold than on a forex pair

A trader who learned on EURUSD carries a habit into gold without noticing it. On EURUSD, 1.0 lot with a 30 pip stop risks about $300, and the stop sits maybe 30 points away on the chart. On XAUUSD, 1.0 lot with a stop 30 dollars away risks $3,000. The lot size looks the same, the number of pips looks familiar, and the risk is ten times bigger.

Gold's daily range does the rest. A normal gold day can move $20 to $40. Anyone carrying FX sizing intuition onto that range will place a stop that a single quiet afternoon clears, then double the size to make the stop feel safer, which doubles the loss per dollar of move.

This is the point where most accounts break: not on a losing trade, but on a lot size that was never priced against the instrument's actual range. Once you know a $0.10 move is $10 per lot, the sentence "I risk 1% per trade" becomes arithmetic instead of a wish.

Using pip value to size a gold trade

Take a $1,000 account risking 1%, so $10 per trade. The stop needs $4.00 of room on the chart. Value of $1.00 of move per lot is 1 × 100 = $100, so $4.00 of room costs $400 per lot. Divide the $10 budget by $400 and the ceiling is 0.025 lots, which rounds down to 0.02 lots. Real risk is $8, inside the plan.

Read the same numbers on a 2% risk budget and the answer is 0.05 lots, which is where most traders start and then keep. The difference between a survivable month and a blown account is usually that one step, and it is a division you can do before the entry instead of after. The lot size calculator runs the same division the other way, from risk to lots, if that is the direction you prefer.

Common questions

Is one pip on gold 0.10 or 0.01?

Both labels are in use, which is why forum numbers disagree. Most brokers and most gold traders call a $0.10 price move one pip, and a $0.01 move one point or pipette. Some brokers, and most MT5 terminals set to three decimals, call the $0.01 move the pip. This page works from the raw price distance in dollars, so the label cannot break the math: 1.0 lot is always $10 per $0.10 and $1 per $0.01.

What is the pip value of 0.01 lots on gold?

0.01 lots is one troy ounce. A $0.10 move is worth $0.10 and a $0.01 move is worth $0.01. That is the smallest size most brokers allow, and it is why a $500 account can only ever risk a few dollars per trade on gold, whatever the leverage shown in the terminal.

My account is not in USD. What changes?

The pip value is in USD because gold is priced in USD. If your account is in another currency, multiply by the rate your broker uses: at 1 USD equals 4.70 MYR, a $10 pip value is 47.00 MYR. The rate field above defaults to 1, so leave it alone unless you need the conversion. Brokers apply their own rate and a small spread on it, so treat the converted figure as a close estimate.

Does leverage change the pip value?

No. Leverage only changes the margin your broker locks when the position opens. The money lost when price moves against you depends on lot size and distance alone, so a 1:500 account and a 1:50 account lose exactly the same amount on the same trade. Higher leverage buys you more room to be wrong, never a cheaper loss.

Why is gold pip value so different from a forex pair?

A standard EURUSD lot is 100,000 units with a pip of 0.0001, which lands near $10 per pip with stops measured in tens of pips. A standard gold lot is 100 ounces where a $0.10 move is already $10, so the same money sits behind a stop that is far tighter in percentage terms. The dollars per pip look familiar while the volatility underneath is not, and that mismatch is exactly where size mistakes start.
